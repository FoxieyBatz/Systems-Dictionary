DiD/OSDD Systems Dictionary
Dissociative Identity Disorder(DiD): formerly known as Multiple Personality Disorder(MPD), is a complex mental health condition often stemming from severe childhood trauma. It is characterized by the presence of two or more distinct alternating identity & personality states of consciousness (often called "ALTERS") and severe memory gaps regarding daily events and personal information. 

Core Symptoms
Distinct Identities: The person has multiple, distinct personality states that alternately take control of their behavior. These "alters" can have unique names, voices, ages, and personal histories.
Dissociative Amnesia: Frequent gaps in memory occur concerning personal history, everyday events, and past traumatic events.
Intrusion of Voices: Individuals may feel as though there are multiple voices, thoughts, or actions in their head that do not belong to them.
Causes
DID is typically a severe coping mechanism developed to deal with overwhelming or continuous trauma, most commonly experienced during early childhood. By dissociating, the mind compartmentalizes the traumatic experience.
Treatment
While there is no "cure" for DID, the condition can be highly manageable with professional support: 

Cleveland Clinic
Psychotherapy: This is the primary treatment. Therapists specialize in helping the person safely process past trauma, integrate the different identity & personality states into a cohesive whole, and develop healthy coping mechanisms.
Medication: There are no medications that specifically treat DID, but psychiatric medication may be used to manage associated symptoms like severe anxiety, depression, or PTSD.

Otherwise Specified Dissociative Disorder(OSDD):
OSDD stands for Other Specified Dissociative Disorder. It is a psychiatric diagnosis for pathological dissociation that causes significant distress or daily impairment, but does not fit the full, rigid criteria of other dissociative disorders like Dissociative Identity Disorder (DID).
OSDD is categorized into specific presentations:
Mixed Dissociative Symptoms (OSDD-1): The most common presentation. It mimics DID (having multiple distinct personality states or "alters") but falls short of DID criteria in one of two ways:
OSDD-1a: The personality states are not fully distinct or separate, but there is significant amnesia or memory loss.
OSDD-1b: The personality states are highly distinct and separate, but there is little to no amnesia between them.
Identity Disturbance due to Coercive Persuasion: Symptoms resulting from prolonged periods of intense manipulation, such as being in a cult or subjected to intense interrogation.
Acute Dissociative Reactions: Sudden, temporary dissociative symptoms triggered by highly stressful, acute events.
Dissociative Trance: An acute narrowing or complete loss of awareness of one's surroundings that is not part of a cultural or religious practice.
Common Symptoms
Individuals with OSDD may experience:
Depersonalization: Feeling detached from one's own body, as if observing oneself from the outside.
Derealization: Feeling that one's surroundings are unreal, dreamlike, or distorted.
Amnesia: Gaps in memory regarding personal history, people, or daily events.
Internal alters: Experiencing an internal system of distinct identities or fragments that influence thoughts, emotions, or behaviors without necessarily taking full executive control of the body.
Causes
Like other complex dissociative disorders, OSDD is rooted in severe, repeated trauma or overwhelming stress experienced during early childhood, when the brain is developing a unified sense of self.

Treatment
Treatment for OSDD is specialized and typically requires long-term psychotherapy with a trauma-informed mental health professional. The most common approaches include: 
Internal Communication: Fostering cooperation, communication, and harmony between different identity states.
Trauma Processing: Utilizing therapies like EMDR or IFS to help the brain reprocess the underlying traumatic memories.
Grounding Techniques: Learning skills to manage severe dissociation, flashbacks, and daily triggers.

Identity versus Personality is an extremely important distinction to start with.
Personality and identity are deeply intertwined psychological concepts, but they serve different roles. Personality represents your inborn and developed behavioral patterns, traits, and emotional responses. Identity is your conscious sense of self, shaped by your values, life stories, group affiliations, and personal ideologies. 
While personality describes how you act, identity dictates who you perceive yourself to be and what you stand for.

The Difference At-A-Glance
Personality: Built on stable traits. It encompasses emotional reactions, habits, and temperament. It answers the question, "How do I behave?"
Identity: Built on self-reflection, values, and social roles. It includes your convictions, career, cultural background, and moral compass. It answers the question, "Who am I?" 
The Five Factor Model (The "Big Five")
Personality is frequently categorized in psychology by the Big Five traits, which measure inherent behavioral baselines: 
Openness: Willingness to try new experiences and think creatively.
Conscientiousness: Your level of organization, dependability, and goal orientation.
Extraversion: How energized you are by social interaction versus solitude.
Agreeableness: Your capacity for empathy, cooperation, and compassion.
Neuroticism: Your emotional stability and reactivity to stress. 
How Identity and Personality Interact:
Your identity acts as a guiding framework for your personality, and your personality helps you express your identity to the world. 

The Actor and the Role: A popular psychological analogy compares personality to an actor, and identity to the specific roles they choose to play in life (e.g., being a parent, a professional, or an artist).
Personality Stability: Research shows that while your underlying personality traits are fairly stable, your identity is constantly shaped and reshaped by life experiences, evolving roles, and shifting priorities. 

Understanding the distinction allows you to evolve your behaviors (personality) to adapt to new environments without losing sight of your core sense of self (identity).
